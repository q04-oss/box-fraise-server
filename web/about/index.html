About

Box Fraise is a project about solving the username and password problem.

The next generation Playboy magazine, and business school. Both at once, and the comparison is one of kind. Playboy was a magazine that was also a scene, a set of clubs, a look, and an argument about how to live — all carried under one name.

Building a community is the point of a business school. That is what the tuition actually buys: not the syllabus, the people you meet in the room. Box Fraise takes that as the whole of it, and adds one thing — it cares about the nervous system of its community, and about nothing else.

Why the nervous system. Because the ultimate goal of a business school is to produce entrepreneurs who are good people, and that is a philosophical claim rather than a curricular one. No course delivers it. So instead of teaching it, Box Fraise orients the community around doing things that are good for the nervous system — running on a Saturday morning, standing at an espresso bar, turning up somewhere at 7:40 because other people will be there too.

What is actually studied. Advertising, and fiction. Advertising because the people who make it are building the world everyone else lives inside, whether or not anyone agreed to live there — and the ones doing that should hold considered opinions about the world rather than none at all. Fiction because that is where those opinions come from. Well-rounded is not a decoration on the entrepreneur; it is the qualification.

The question a business school actually asks. How do you sell a product. Everything else is downstream of it. And the student sitting in the room is both the victim and the prey — advertised to their whole life, and now being taught to do the advertising. How they imagine advertising works is what decides what they end up making.

A product is a response to a community. How someone sees themselves inside one informs what they build for it. So the route to a better product is not a better module on product design; it is a better person. Make someone the best version of themselves and the thing they make changes with them.

Trust is the precondition. Among people who trust each other, the real conversation about advertising becomes possible — the one that cannot be had honestly anywhere else. And an entrepreneur inside a community that already exists is not solving distribution. There is no problem of getting in touch with anyone, because the people are already there.

What an advertisement could be. Where it is safe to connect, the best advertising happens — and the best advertising connects to a person's entire self, not to the sliver of them that was up for sale. That is what all of this is arranged to make possible.

The reading is the networking. Not a seminar with drinks afterwards. Loving a book and arguing about it with other people is itself the environment where the network forms — the same way the run is. Nobody has to be introduced to anybody.

The run club. Every Saturday, Box Fraise runs a club out of Coffee Bureau, timed to the opening of the cafe. You do not need to be fast and you do not need to know anyone.

The problem. A username and a password are what you use when there is nobody there to recognise you. They stand in for being known. Every account is a small act of proving yourself to a machine, repeated forever, and the whole apparatus exists because the digital world has no equivalent of walking into a room where people already know your face.

The approach. Box Fraise solves it in person, and it does it playfully. Verification happens face to face, at events, between people — an in-person design structure rather than a login screen. The playfulness is not decoration. A structure people enjoy taking part in is one they will actually turn up to, and turning up is the entire mechanism.

A friendship network. Because that in-person element is community-oriented, the accurate name for what it produces is not a social network but a friendship network. Membership is not a form you fill in. It is people meeting other people, with the software keeping faithful track of the fact that it happened.

Consent and accountability. As a platform, its behaviour is to create an environment where it is possible to consensually call people out — accountability that people have opted into, rather than accountability imposed by an anonymous crowd. That is what lets the physical world and the digital one sit in harmony instead of in tension. The consequence is physical communities reinforced by being online rather than hollowed out by it.

The app that does not exist yet. There will be an iOS app. There isn't one, for a reason worth saying out loud rather than dressing up: the founder cannot afford a Macintosh, and you cannot build for iPhone without one. So the website is the whole product for now, which has turned out to be the right constraint — nobody has to install anything to take part.

What it will look like. Maps. A map with markers, and a marker on Coffee Bureau saying the run club will be there. That is the first version, and it is deliberately not much more than that.

Every cafe needs a run club. That is the argument the first version of the app is built to make. The espresso bar is one of the few genuinely democratic meeting spaces left: anyone can stand at it, and standing at it is enough to be part of what is happening there. A run club gives a cafe a reason to gather people rather than only serve them.

A login for everyone else. In time Box Fraise will publish its own login API, so that other projects can authenticate people through the network instead of issuing usernames and passwords of their own. Someone verified in person, once, becomes someone any participating project can trust. That is what solving the problem is for — not one platform with a better login, but a way of being known that other people's software can build on.

The founder. He has always wondered what it would take to imagine a business school like this, and this is the attempt to find out by building it rather than writing it down. He is also an advertising nerd, and will talk about advertising for as long as you let him.

On it seeming new. The world we live in now has not been through an update like this one, so parts of it will read as unfamiliar. Nothing here is exotic, though. It is the obvious next thing, and someone was going to arrive at it eventually.
